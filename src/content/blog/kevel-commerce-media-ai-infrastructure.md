A growing number of retailers are quietly becoming publishers. They run their own ad networks on their own sites, selling sponsored placements to brands that want to reach shoppers at the moment of decision. The space has a name — commerce media or retail media — and it is one of the fastest-growing corners of digital advertising.

The latest signal of how serious this is: Kevel, the API-first ad-tech infrastructure provider that powers commerce media networks, announced a **$52M funding round** — its largest to date — to scale AI-driven infrastructure for the industry. The announcement, reported by The AI Insider, brings Kevel's total funding above $100M, with participation from Fulcrum Equity Partners, dunnhumby Ventures, Iberis Capital, and growth capital from ORIX USA. Per the report, Kevel already powers more than 100 commerce media networks, including Tesco, Dollar General Media Networks, John Lewis Partnership, PayPal, and Home Depot's Orange Apron Media.

For content-ops and commerce teams, this is more than a funding headline. It is a useful lens on a structural shift: retailers are moving from merely running sites to operating media businesses — and AI infrastructure is becoming the engine that makes that possible.

## What commerce media actually is

Commerce media is advertising that lives on a retailer's own digital properties — search results, product pages, category pages, emails, even off-site placements like display and social, driven by a retailer's shopper data. The retailer plays the role of both merchant and publisher: it controls the inventory, the audience, and the data about what shoppers actually buy.

What makes retail media attractive to brands is the first-party data. A brand advertising on a retail site can reach someone who is demonstrably in market — they are on the site, browsing a category, close to a purchase. That intent signal is far stronger than the broad demographic targeting available in much of the open web. Retailers, for their part, get a high-margin revenue stream that does not require holding more inventory, and they get to monetize traffic they already have.

## Why retailers are building ad businesses

Historically, most retailers left advertising to intermediaries. A site would slot in ads served by third-party networks, collecting a small slice of the revenue and little of the data. The walled-garden platforms — the large marketplaces and social networks — captured most of the value because they owned the audience, the measurement, and the ad stack.

Two forces changed the calculus:

- **The end of the third-party cookie.** As browsers and privacy regulation eroded third-party tracking, the value of a retailer's own first-party data rose sharply. Retailers now hold something advertisers badly need: logged-in, purchase-verified audiences.
- **The revenue opportunity.** Retail media has grown into a multi-billion-dollar market, and it is one of the few advertising categories growing while the rest of the ecosystem matures. Brands are actively shifting budget toward retailer media networks because the data is better.

The result is that retailers want to own their monetization rather than rent it. That is exactly the framing Kevel CEO James Avery used in the funding announcement: "Businesses must own their monetization and data" to compete with the largest platforms. The infrastructure layer is what lets a retailer run a media network without building an entire ad platform from scratch.

## The AI infrastructure layer

Running a media network is not trivial. It involves ad serving, targeting, ranking, auction, measurement, reporting — plus the unique constraint of doing all of it inside a retailer's own brand experience, where a bad ad placement can hurt conversion and shopper trust.

This is where AI infrastructure comes in. Modern commerce media relies on machine-learning models that decide which ad to show to which shopper, at what price, in which placement, in milliseconds, while balancing competing goals: relevance to the shopper, return for the advertiser, and revenue for the retailer.

Kevel's announced focus is characterized as "transparent AI" — decisioning that is explainable, auditable, and configurable by the retailer. The rationale is straightforward: if a retailer cannot see or control how ad decisions are made, it cannot prove performance to advertisers, protect the shopper experience, or keep ownership of its data and models. This matters for content teams too: if an AI is deciding placements, you want to be able to explain why a certain product got featured, both for advertisers and for regulatory goodwill.

The point is that retailers do not need to become AI companies. They need infrastructure that lets them configure and trust the AI that powers their media business, while bringing their own data and models if they choose.

## What this means for commerce and content teams

Whether your team runs a retail site, a marketplace, or a commerce-focused content business, a few takeaways follow.

**First-party data is the strategic asset.** The moat here is the data you already have — what shoppers search for, view, and buy. Structuring that data, keeping it clean, and being able to activate it for audiences is the foundation of any media network. This mirrors the broader push toward first-party strategies across content and commerce.

**AI is a configuration problem, not a black box.** The credible providers are not selling a mysterious algorithm; they are selling decisioning you can inspect and control. When evaluating infrastructure, ask whether you can see why a placement was chosen, override bad decisions, and audit results. "Explainable" is becoming a procurement requirement.

**Healthy ads depend on healthy content.** A retail media network lives inside pages that shoppers actually trust. If your search and product pages are thin, broken, or slow, ads will not rescue them — they will make the experience worse. Strong commerce content is the substrate that makes ad inventory valuable in the first place.

Publishing is becoming a core competence for retailers. The funding momentum behind infrastructure players like Kevel signals that the industry believes this is a lasting shift, not a cycle. For teams building or operating these networks, the formula is consistent: own your data, make your AI decisions transparent and controllable, and never let monetization degrade the experience that earns the audience.
